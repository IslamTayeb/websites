I used to play this rhythm game called [osu!](https://osu.ppy.sh/home) from ages 11 to 17. You might've seen [clips](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Cp9pyCFRyJ0) of people reacting to it. The concept is simple: you click circles to the beat of a song. Most players aim with a graphic tablet and tap with Z + X on their keyboard. I played semi-competitively around 2020-2022, chasing maps no one in the country had aced.

After years of dramatically telling friends about how much this game changed my life, I dusted off my tablet and dug up my old settings. I started inviting friends to my dorm to try it on my setup. I expected 3 or 4 to show up. **22 people did!** Most were flailing the pen while circles zoomed past them and having a good time (I hope). But a few kept coming back and noticeably improved, and watching them made me think about how people get better.

I started osu! with zero rhythm knowledge. Never played an instrument. Never played any games competitively. I could barely pass 2-star maps with a D rank. Thinking about what I was doing felt like too much effort, so I just shut my brain off and clicked. When I saw a hard pattern, I'd decide I was guaranteed to miss 1-2 circles and just accept it since I'd still pass. I eventually got better, but this habit carried on until I was top 5K global.

Then I met [bloo](https://osu.ppy.sh/users/6778877) and [eternum](https://osu.ppy.sh/users/4581069), who caught on and told me to care about every circle and detail. I stayed in voice chat with bloo retrying the same pattern 100+ times until I pinned down what it's like to take that pattern "one circle at a time." It felt wrong, like when someone reminds you that you're breathing and suddenly you're breathing manually. But with time, I got better.

With my friends, some fixes were mechanical. A lot of people kept their wrist hovering above the tablet, and planting it on the desk made their aim more stable immediately. A hovering wrist caps your potential no matter how much you practice. Others were mental: a lot of people reacted to each circle as it appeared. Once I pointed out that the tempo is consistent and already tells you when the next circle is coming, they stopped reacting and started anticipating. How fast you improve depends on how much you care, and most people don't care enough to improve on a silly circle-clicking game. That's completely fine.

Though I have to hold myself from [backseat gaming](https://www.urbandictionary.com/define.php?term=Backseat%20Gaming) or [mansplaining](https://www.urbandictionary.com/define.php?term=mansplain), I find it hard to give solid feedback on how to improve. [Fingerspitzengefühl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingerspitzengef%C3%BChl) (finger-shpit-sen-guh-fool) is German for "fingertip feeling." It's the intuitive sense of what to do that comes from experience. I can feel when my aim is too stiff or my tapping is off-tempo, but I can't fully explain how I know or how I adjust my aim or tapping. Even though there are many little heuristics I tacitly know but can't communicate (e.g. certain angles that keep a stable grip), best I can say in the moment is "your aim is too unstable."

Even those 5 words can be interpreted differently. I've seen people aim slower or start dragging, both of which aren't quite "stabilizing" their aim. So there are seemingly 2 considerations: (1) saying the right thing, and (2) interpreting it. I think bloo's advice only landed because I had *just* enough self-awareness to feel what he meant by "complacency."

You can't transfer fingerspitzengefühl through language. The best you can do is point someone in the right direction.
